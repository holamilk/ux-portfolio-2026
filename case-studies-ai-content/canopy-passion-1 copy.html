What happens when research kills your idea

At a glance

The problem

People can't reach national parks — disability, distance, cost, time. Meanwhile the parks they can reach are overcrowded. NPS has no way to deliver a park experience to someone who can't get to one.

The solution

Canopy: an immersive pod placed in urban communities, with booking and recap inside the NPS app. 360° projections, spatial audio, interactive floor. Explore Mode for discovery, Meditate Mode for restoration. Up to four people, wheelchair accessible, free.

Canopy pod concept and NPS app

Discovery

We walked George L. Smith State Park before designing anything. Three benches along three miles. Steep grade changes with no warning. Static signage, no real-time information. One of us walked it in a 12lb weighted vest.

"The grades stopped reading as scenery and started reading as barriers."

Six concepts, ranked — and the ranking inverted our plan.

Social, in-park concepts beat remote and solo ones every time. At-home versions came last by a wide margin. People don't want a park simulated at home. They want a reason to go somewhere.

The pod went from private device to placed, shared, public installation.

Three research questions, held against every decision:

"How do visitors understand accessibility while navigating?"

"Information inside the pod is ambient, not something you hunt for."

"What causes confusion or exclusion?"

"Two modes instead of one, so people choose how they engage."

"Why do current tools fail?"

"Make it physical and legible from outside before you commit to entering."

Design

We simulated a pod we couldn't build — moody lighting, a large screen, seating, tap-to-enter. Crude, but it tested how it feels to sit inside an experience instead of asking people to imagine a render.

"Every tap pulled people out of the forest."

Cause was consistent: touching a screen mid-experience. Fix was structural — interactive moments moved to before and after. Ambient triggers inside. The app stops being a remote the moment you sit down.

We built the trip recap as a summary — distance, species, time. The screens tested flat, but the idea landed hard. People wanted to share it, collect from it. We rebuilt it around badges and challenges. It went from a receipt to a reason to come back.

Smaller fixes along the way:

• Lists that looked clickable but weren't — fixed visual affordances.

• Rebuilt NPS global and local components in Figma so our additions sat inside the system, not beside it.

• Added accessibility controls to booking: contrast, dictation, epileptic content, loud frequencies, strong scents, stable temperature.

• Sensory needs are set before you enter, not negotiated inside.

Behind the pixels

A

Being Scrum Master made me a better designer

Owning the timeline forced me to be specific about what was worth reopening. Scoping the change tightly to the concept while protecting the app work already done is what made the pivot survivable.

B

Immersion is fragile, and it fails at the interaction

Not at content quality. Every moment a user thinks about the interface, the experience collapses. Designing for immersion means knowing where not to put a control.

C

What I'd do differently

More accessibility scenarios. Further iteration on the immersive tech interactions. A real implementation roadmap — we validated the concept convincingly, but not how it gets built.
