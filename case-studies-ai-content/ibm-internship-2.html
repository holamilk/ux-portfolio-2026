Designing Inside an Acquisition

Part One — Redesigning an acquired product
Part Two — Building AI workflows

Stepping into enterprise security architecture during a corporate acquisition to navigate technical debt, fragmented interfaces, and scale interim design frameworks.

IBM zSecure — product overview

The situation

Enterprise software acquisitions live in a peculiar architectural limbo. When a major organization absorbs a specialized security platform, the immediate priority is business continuity rather than immediate design harmonization, leaving users straddling distinct visual and technical paradigms.

Entering the enterprise security division as a design intern meant stepping directly into this friction point, where technical debt and newly inherited codebases dictate every interaction model.

"I spent the summer on two things."
Part One

Redesigning an acquired product

Full unification was years away. This redesign had to be a credible interim state. We could not rewrite the front-end architecture overnight, nor could we force users into an unproven layout structure.

The challenge was finding the precise boundary where visual modernization could occur without breaking the mental models of security administrators who relied on split-second muscle memory during active audits.

legacy vs interim state component mapping

The release decision

Splitting the release would have satisfied early deployment metrics for stakeholders, but it risked fragmenting the user experience right as customers were adjusting to the transition under the new corporate umbrella.

"Ship half early, or ship all of it a few weeks later. We shipped it whole."
Part Two

Building AI workflows

After stabilizing the interim UI, I shifted to exploring opportunities where AI could reduce manual toil for administrators: pattern detection in audit logs, automated alert prioritization, and assisted remediation flows.

AI needed guardrails. We prototyped small, explainable workflows that surfaced recommendations without obscuring the underlying security signals.

The team validated that lightweight automation could reduce mean time to respond (MTTR) while preserving analyst control—an essential balance for security products.

ai workflow prototype and recommendation preview

AI workflows

We shipped three pilot workflows: automated triage, suggested remediation snippets, and anomaly clustering. Each workflow included an explicit "why" panel explaining the model's reasoning to maintain trust.

"Designing for confidence, not surprise."

Early pilots reduced analyst overhead and surfaced non-obvious correlations that previously required manual investigation.

Reflection

Working inside an acquisition taught me how to balance design ambition with operational constraints: small surface-area wins, explicit communication, and incremental automation delivered measurable outcomes.

Product teams that invest in clarity and staged rollouts earn the right to move faster later—this project was an example of that trade-off in practice.
