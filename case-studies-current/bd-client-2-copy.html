The Future of Continence Care

Designing for dignity in a space defined by silence and stigma

Project Manager & UX Designer · SCADpro × Becton Dickinson · 10 weeks

Nobody had ever designed incontinence care as a system.

Overview

Problem
PureWick, an external catheter system that lets women manage urinary incontinence at home without invasive procedures, was already in homes. But the experience around it had never been designed. Fragmented care, no clinical continuity, and social stigma meant users were underserved at every touchpoint.

The business challenge

Continence care is highly fragmented. Social stigma makes incontinence a hidden disability: people are underdiagnosed, underinformed, and often only discover solutions incidentally while treating something else. Digital tools exist but adoption is inconsistent, and broader education around the topic is largely absent.

BD's objective was clear: map the current care journey, uncover unmet needs, and build a scalable service design framework that embeds experience-led thinking across the organization. PureWick was already in homes. Design was the missing capability to make it actually work there.

Research methods

Incontinence is a topic people don't talk about openly — which meant standard research methods wouldn't get us far. We interviewed three groups deliberately: caregivers managing a loved one's care at home, previous patients who could articulate what the system got wrong, and medical professionals who gave us the systemic view of where the handoff breaks down.

We also ran semantic differential surveys — measuring emotional and perceptual responses rather than asking direct questions. We wanted to understand what words, visuals, packaging, and imagery people associate with stigma and discomfort versus confidence and autonomy. With a topic this charged, how someone responds to the word "incontinence" tells you more than what they say they need.

Example of a semantic differential survey result

What the research told us

Clinicians prescribe. DME suppliers ship. An untrained family caregiver becomes responsible for a medical device at home with no clinical support. Nobody in that chain owns the experience.

We used a five-whys root cause analysis to get beneath the surface. The root cause: loss of bodily control erodes dignity and identity, leading to shame, withdrawal, and emotional distress that compound over time.

Root causes
  1. 01

    Dignity is non-negotiable — loss of bodily control erodes identity and autonomy, and care systems that ignore this cause as much harm as the condition itself.

  2. 02

    One-size-fits-all production ignores the person behind the case — products built on averages create poor fit, dignity loss, and caregiver strain.

  3. 03

    Asking for help is seen as personal failure — generational ideologies make people resist care long past the point they need it.

  4. 04

    The condition is taboo before it is a problem — social stigma means people normalize symptoms, joke about them, and never talk about them seriously until a crisis.

  5. 05

    The healthcare system is built around efficiency, not the person — profit and generalization drive surface-level care that misses interconnected needs.

The emotional journey made this visible. Users in pre-awareness and awareness stages felt anxiety and discomfort. Even after adopting the product, the onboarding phase dropped back into confusion, leaving users far below where they needed to be. The north star was simple: I am respected. Three pillars made that real: feeling dignified, safe, and supported.

The ecosystem we designed for

The patient was rarely the only user. We mapped the full picture: patient, family caregiver, home health aide, DME supplier, and discharging clinician, and treated every touchpoint as a design opportunity.

How we approached it

This wasn't about adding features to a product. It was about designing the entire experience of becoming a PureWick user, before, during, and after adoption, across three service design layers.

My role

I ran the project and shaped the design thinking inside it.

Project management

As PM, I built the sprint plan backward from each client check-in, managed five cross-functional subteams: research, service design, UX, advertising, and documentary, and built a FigJam kanban from scratch when our project management tooling fell short.

Design contribution

As a designer, I contributed to research synthesis, journey mapping, and service design output. My job was making sure every subteam's work connected back to one strategic thesis and that what we presented reflected the depth of thinking behind it.
