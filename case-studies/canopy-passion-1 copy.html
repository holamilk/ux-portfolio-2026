Canopy: Bringing National Parks to You

An immersive urban pod for the people who can't make the trip

Scrum Master & Experience Design · SCAD Studio · 5 designers · 10 weeks

Canopy pod concept render or NPS app hi-fi screens
A national park is three hours away. You have two kids, one day off, and no car. It stays three hours away.

Overview

Problem
Millions of people can't experience national parks because of distance, cost, time, or physical barriers — while the parks they can reach grow more crowded. NPS has no way to deliver a park experience to someone who can't make the trip.
Solution
Canopy is an immersive pod placed in urban communities that recreates a national park through 360° projections, spatial audio, and an interactive floor — bookable and recapped through the NPS app. Free, wheelchair accessible, up to four people.

Discovery

We walked the park before designing for it.

We couldn't even get to a national park to do our research. So we went to George L. Smith State Park — which, honestly, proved the point before we said a word. Three benches along three miles. Steep grade changes with no warning. Static signage, no real-time information. One of us walked it in a 12lb weighted vest to understand what a physical barrier actually feels like mid-trail.

The grades stopped reading as scenery and started reading as barriers.

Access isn't just a trail problem.

Our research kept pointing to the same thing. The barriers start long before anyone reaches a trailhead. Distance. Cost. No time off. A family of five that can't coordinate a three-hour drive. An older parent who can't handle uneven terrain. A person in a wheelchair for whom most park infrastructure was never designed.

The parks that are reachable are overcrowded. The ones worth visiting stay out of reach.

Six concepts, ranked — and the ranking inverted our plan.

We tested six directions. Social, placed, in-community concepts won every time. At-home simulations — which we had spent weeks developing — came dead last by a wide margin.

People don't want a park simulated at home. They want a reason to leave the house and go somewhere together.

We cut the at-home direction entirely and reframed the pod as a shared public destination, not a private device.

six concepts ranked chart and concept reactions

Three research questions held against every decision.

  • How do visitors understand accessibility while navigating? Information inside the pod is ambient, not something you hunt for.
  • What causes confusion or exclusion? Two modes instead of one — Explore for discovery, Meditate for restoration — so people choose how they engage.
  • Why do current tools fail? Make it physical and legible from outside before you commit to entering.

Design

We simulated a pod we couldn't build.

Moody lighting, a large screen running the content, seating, tap-to-enter. Crude, but it tested how it feels to sit inside an experience rather than asking people to imagine a render.

Every tap pulled people out of the forest.

Participants said the experience started well, then dropped. Cause was consistent: touching a screen mid-experience broke immersion every time. Some weren't sure whether to interact at all. The fix was structural — interactive moments moved to before and after. Ambient triggers inside. The app stops being a remote the moment you sit down.

Every tap pulled people out of the forest.

The recap went from a receipt to a reason to come back.

We built the trip summary first — distance walked, species spotted, time spent. The screens tested flat but the idea landed. People wanted to share it, collect from it, show their kids. We rebuilt it around badges and challenges. One structural change turned a dead-end screen into the most engaging part of the app.

Smaller fixes that mattered.

  • Lists that looked clickable but weren't — fixed visual affordances
  • Rebuilt NPS global and local Figma components so our additions sat inside the system, not beside it
  • Added accessibility controls to booking — contrast, dictation, epileptic content, loud frequencies, strong scents, stable temperature. Sensory needs are set before you enter, not negotiated inside.

My role

I was Scrum Master — sprint planning, two parallel workstreams across pod and app, a ten-week timeline that survived a mid-project pivot. I also contributed to experience design, the mode structure, the in-pod interaction model, and naming and visual direction.

Owning the timeline forced me to be specific about what was worth reopening. Scoping the pivot tightly — cutting the at-home concept while protecting the app work already done — is what made it survivable.

What I'd do differently

More accessibility scenarios in testing. Further iteration on the immersive tech interactions. A real implementation roadmap — we validated the concept convincingly, but not how it actually gets built.
