The business challenge

PureWick had established itself as the best product in its category. But the competitive landscape was shifting — new entrants were actively positioning themselves as the next generation beyond PureWick. The real problem wasn't the product. It was the system around it.

"The real problem wasn't the product. It was the system around it."

Incontinence care had never been designed as a coherent experience. Clinicians prescribe. DME suppliers ship. And then an untrained family caregiver — with no clinical support — becomes responsible for managing a medical device at home. The result: a fragmented journey, poor adoption, and a product that often failed not because of what it was, but because of how it landed.

The critical insight

Incontinence care is a prime example of bad service design — because nobody designs it as a system.

The emotional journey reflected this. Users in the pre-awareness and awareness stages experienced discomfort and anxiety. Even after adoption, the onboarding phase dropped back into confusion — leaving users stuck at fair when the goal was ideal.

The ideal user outcome, grounded in research, was clear: I am respected. Three pillars made that real — feeling dignified, safe, and supported.

Our approach

We mapped the full ecosystem: the patient, the family caregiver (often the real primary user), the home health aide, the DME supplier, and the discharging clinician. Each touchpoint represented an opportunity to intervene.

Frontstage

More personalized interactions that account for who the target audience actually interacts with — family, HCPs, and peer communities.

Backstage

Optimized internal processes and streamlined communication between BD, DME suppliers, and clinical partners.

Support Process

Scalable, long-term infrastructure that grows alongside demand.

My role

"My job was coherence."

As Project Manager, I owned the structure and momentum of the entire engagement. I built and ran the sprint plan backward from each client check-in, managed cross-functional workstreams across five subteams, and ran our FigJam-based kanban to track delivery.

Beyond logistics, my job was coherence — making sure the story we told in the room reflected the depth of thinking behind it, and that every subteam's output connected back to a single strategic thesis.

Outcome

The final presentation to BD's 12-person stakeholder group delivered a complete service design framework with projected market acquisition targets:
