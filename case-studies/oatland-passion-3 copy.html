Oatland Island Wildlife Center Redesign

Simplifying an information dense website so visitors can prepare best for a wildlife visit

Lead UX/UI Strategist · SCAD Studio · 4 designers · 10 weeks

Turning a cluttered wildlife website into one people can actually navigate

Overview

Problem
Poor navigation, inconsistent hierarchy, and disorganized content made it hard for visitors to find basic information — hours, events, programs — before giving up.
What we did
Restructured the information architecture, modernized the visual design, and prioritized accessibility. Task completion dropped from 27 seconds to 9 seconds.

Discovery

The navigation was the root of everything.

A heuristic evaluation and as-is sitemap made the problem visible immediately — too many redundant categories, no clear hierarchy, and a second navigation header that sent users off to external sites with no way back.

original website
as-is sitemap showing category sprawl

We went to the park before redesigning the website.

We observed families moving through the space — most were there for recreation and had no idea educational programs existed. We also ran card sorting with 10 participants. Users consistently wanted fewer top-level categories. The existing structure reflected how the organization thought about itself, not how visitors looked for things.

family visit observation
park field research

What the research told us.

  • Visitors couldn't find key information fast enough
  • Education is a core business priority — but users didn't know programs existed
  • Target audience spans families, schools, and teachers
  • Copy felt institutional, not inviting
  • Users wanted fewer clicks to reach anything important

One tension we had to design through.

The center wanted educational programs more prominent — a clear business priority. But visitors came to the site with one goal first: find hours, see the animals, decide if the trip is worth it. Pushing programs too early would bury what they actually needed.

Our solution did both. Education got its own top-level navigation category — visible to teachers and schools planning field trips. The homepage led with what casual visitors needed first. Users got in fast. The center got its programs in front of the right audience.

Design

Brand identity before screens.

I built the full brand ID and style guide — typography, color, iconography, voice and tone, and imagery direction. Friendly and approachable for families, with educational information prominent enough for teachers. Rounded edges, warm nature palette, real photography.

Oatland style guide
brand moodboard

Real copy from the start.

We avoided lorem ipsum in wireframes. Real copy meant we could test whether anyone could find event info or book tickets in under 3 clicks. The original site read like an internal document. We rewrote it to sound like the center wanted people to visit. I also built an SEO strategy alongside — embedding high-traffic wildlife search terms into headers, navigation labels, and body copy.

Two features that weren't in the brief.

I built an interactive trail map and used paw print icons to separate animal species. Both came from watching families move through the park during field research. They were the most engaged-with features in usability testing.

The best features came from watching, not assuming.

Testing

Formative testing confirmed the new navigation worked and flagged two things to fix — dropdown contrast and imagery. We fixed both.

Final testing came back 3x faster across all tasks. SUS scores above benchmark. Users said the site felt welcoming in a way the original never did.

What I took from this

When users win, the business does too. Making the site easier to navigate meant more program bookings, less frustration, and a digital presence that actually reflects what the center is. Every design decision carried weight beyond the screen — that's the lens I bring to every project now.
